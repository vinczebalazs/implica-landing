Daily news for students: the best sources by age

A good student news source is accurate, written for its age group, and explains instead of assuming. Here are the best current-events sources for each stage, from early readers to college, plus tips for building a daily news habit.

Updated 15 Oct 2026 By Implica 6 min read

THE SHORT ANSWER

For younger students, News-O-Matic, Time for Kids, Scholastic News and The Week Junior are written for their age. For mixed reading levels, Newsela offers the same story at several levels. For middle and high school, CNN 10 is a ten-minute daily video, and The New York Times' Learning Network and PBS NewsHour Classroom add free lesson plans. For college and older students, move on to adult news: AP News, a daily newsletter or podcast, or an app that explains the context, such as Implica (which we make).

What makes a good student news source

Most adult news assumes you already know the background: who the people are, what happened last month, why a vote or a ceasefire matters. That's exactly what students often don't know yet. The Reuters Institute's 2025 Digital News Report found that news avoiders aged 18 to 24 are more likely than those over 55 to say they avoid the news because it's hard to understand or doesn't feel relevant to them.

So beyond accuracy, look for sources that:

  • Are written for the reader's age and reading level.
  • Explain context: why a story matters and how it got here.
  • Separate reporting from opinion and label opinion clearly.
  • Name their sources, so students learn to check where information comes from.
  • Have an end. A short daily set beats an endless feed for building a habit.

Elementary school

News-O-Matic

News-O-Matic publishes daily news articles written for children from kindergarten to eighth grade. Each story comes at several reading levels, with a read-aloud option and translations into languages including Spanish, French and Mandarin. It's built for classrooms, priced by quote for schools, and offers a free trial.

Time for Kids

From the publishers of TIME, Time for Kids offers separate editions for kindergarten and first grade, second grade, grades 3–4 and grades 5–6, in print and online, with teaching resources. It's a subscription, usually bought by schools.

Scholastic News

Scholastic's classroom magazines are a staple in US schools. Scholastic News comes in grade-specific editions for elementary students, and the series continues with Junior Scholastic for grades 6–8 and The New York Times Upfront for grades 9–12. They're sold to schools as classroom subscriptions.

The Week Junior

The Week Junior is a weekly print news magazine for readers aged 8 to 14, delivered to the home. It covers world events, science, sport and culture in a friendly, balanced way. A weekly format suits children who'd find daily news too much.

Middle school

Newsela

Newsela takes real news articles and rewrites each at several reading levels, so a whole class, from roughly grade 2 to grade 12, can read the same story at the level that suits each student. It includes quizzes and writing prompts. Some content is free, and the full platform is a paid school subscription.

Junior Scholastic, covered above, is the other classic choice for this age.

High school

CNN 10

CNN 10, which used to be called CNN Student News, is a ten-minute daily video covering the major stories, made for middle and high school classrooms. It's free, and it works well as a daily warm-up during the school year.

The New York Times Learning Network

The Learning Network builds lessons around real New York Times journalism, with daily writing prompts, discussion questions, lesson plans and student contests. It's free, including the Times articles it links to, and it's one of the richest resources for teachers.

PBS NewsHour Classroom

PBS NewsHour Classroom, which replaced NewsHour Extra in 2021, offers free daily news lessons, lesson plans and short videos built around current stories for grades 6–12, including media-literacy material.

Student News Daily

Student News Daily is a long-running site that publishes a daily news article for students, alongside world-events and editorial sections. It's simple, free to read, and works as a ready-made daily current-events task.

Current events for college and older students

By the late teens, students are ready for adult news. The challenge is that adult news rarely explains itself. A few good routes:

  • A wire service. AP News is free and reports straightforwardly. It's excellent for facts, though light on background. Reuters is similar but needs a subscription.
  • A daily newsletter or podcast. A free morning briefing, such as the 1440 newsletter or NPR's Up First podcast, covers the day's main stories in a few minutes and then ends.
  • An app that explains the context. See Implica below, or compare options in the best news apps and the five types of news aggregator.

Building a daily news habit

  • Make it a fixed slot. Five or ten minutes at the same time each day (a class warm-up, breakfast, the bus) works better than "whenever".
  • One story, discussed, beats ten skimmed. Ask: What happened? Why does it matter? Who is affected? What happened before?
  • Practise checking sources. Ask where a claim comes from, and whether another outlet reports it the same way.
  • Mix in good news and solutions. A diet of only crises can leave young people feeling powerless, and feeling worn down by the news is one of the main reasons people give for avoiding it.
  • Keep it finite. Endless feeds are a poor first news habit. See how to stop doomscrolling.

Questions people ask

What is the best free news source for students?

CNN 10, The New York Times Learning Network and PBS NewsHour Classroom are all free and made for students. For older students, AP News is free and reliable.

How can students get unbiased news?

No source is perfectly unbiased, so the best skill is comparing. Tools like AllSides show left, centre and right headlines side by side, and wire services like AP and Reuters stick closely to facts. See our guide to tools for balanced news.

How much news should a student read each day?

A few minutes daily is enough to build the habit. Understanding one story well is worth more than skimming many headlines.
