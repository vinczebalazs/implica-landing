THE SHORT ANSWER

To see every side of a story, use Ground News or AllSides. For straight, fact-first reporting, use a wire service: AP News is free, and Reuters needs a subscription. No app removes bias entirely, so the most reliable approach is to combine a fact-first source with one that shows you how coverage differs.

What "unbiased" can realistically mean

Bias in news rarely means made-up facts. More often it's in the choices: which stories get covered, which get ignored, which quotes lead, which words describe the same event ("protesters" or "rioters", "tax relief" or "tax cuts"). Because every outlet has to make those choices, no source is perfectly neutral.

What you can find are sources that reduce bias in one of three ways:

  • Showing every side: putting coverage from across the political spectrum next to each other, so you can see the differences.
  • Sticking to facts: reporting what happened with minimal framing, as wire services do.
  • Summarising without opinion: condensing many outlets' reporting into one neutral account.

Apps that show every side

Ground News

GOOD FOR Spotting what each side isn't covering

Ground News is the best-known "unbiased news app". It groups articles about the same event and shows what share of the coverage comes from left, centre and right-leaning outlets. Its "Blindspot" feature highlights stories covered mostly by one side. It also rates each outlet for bias, factuality and ownership. The ratings come from third-party services and are framed around US politics, and the full tools are paid. Price: free tier; paid plans up to $99.99 a year.

AllSides

GOOD FOR Left, centre and right side by side, free

AllSides shows a left, a centre and a right-leaning headline for each big story, next to each other, with a short neutral summary. It also publishes widely used media bias ratings for hundreds of outlets. It's free, and quick to read daily. Price: free.

Google News (Full Coverage)

GOOD FOR The widest range of outlets on one story

Google News doesn't label outlets by lean, but tapping Full Coverage on a story shows it as reported by many different outlets, plus opinion and analysis. That view isn't personalised, so everyone sees the same range. Avoid relying only on its personalised "For you" tab, which learns from what you already read. Price: free.

Fact-first sources

Wire services write news for other outlets to publish, so their reporting is designed to be used by newspapers of every political leaning. That makes their style deliberately plain: what happened, who said what, with little framing.

AP News

GOOD FOR Straight reporting, free

The Associated Press is a US non-profit news cooperative whose reporting runs in thousands of outlets. Its app and website are free. It's strong on facts and breaking news, and light on background. Price: free.

Reuters

GOOD FOR Straight reporting on world and business news

Reuters is a global wire service, especially strong on international and financial news. The media-rating group Ad Fontes Media places it in the middle of its political bias scale and rates it as reliable. Its website and app need a subscription. Price: subscription.

Neutral summaries and briefings

Particle

GOOD FOR AI summaries drawn from many outlets

Particle reads many articles about a story and writes one AI summary, which by design blends sources from across the spectrum. One of its views lays out the opposing sides of an issue. As with any AI summary, check the original reporting when details matter. Price: free; Particle+ $2.99 a month.

1440

GOOD FOR A short, plain daily email

1440 is a free daily email newsletter that summarises the day's news in about five minutes, and markets itself as unbiased and fact-based. It's a popular, readable briefing. That claim is its own, though; we found no independent audit of it. Price: free.

Implica (we make it, so we're biased about this one)

GOOD FOR Context without opinion

FULL DISCLOSURE

We make Implica, which is a little awkward in an article about bias. So we'll stick to what it does, not to claims about it. It doesn't rate outlets or show a left-right split, and we don't call it "unbiased".

Implica's approach is explanation. It gives you a short, curated set of the day's most important stories, each built from the reporting of several outlets and listed with its sources. Each story covers what happened, why it matters and who it affects, the background, a timeline and what to watch next. Implica doesn't set out to express opinions or take editorial positions. Where views appear, they come from the underlying reporting. Its job is to explain the story, not to argue about it.

Why that helps with bias: a lot of one-sided news works by leaving out context. Knowing the background of a story makes slanted framing easier to spot, wherever you read it. Implica covers world, tech and science news, plus a local edition for Hungary in Hungarian and English. It uses AI to summarise and explain, so check important details against the listed sources. Price: free for 14 days, then a subscription.

What is the most unbiased news source?

There's no single answer, but independent rating services broadly agree on a few points:

  • Wire services such as Reuters and AP are consistently rated among the most factual and least slanted, because their reporting has to work for outlets of every leaning.
  • Ratings differ between raters. AllSides, Ad Fontes Media and Media Bias/Fact Check use different methods, and they don't always agree on where an outlet sits. Treat any single rating as a guide, not a verdict.
  • Straight news and opinion differ even within one outlet. A newspaper's news reporting can be far more neutral than its opinion pages.

How to read news less one-sidedly

  • Read one story in two places. Pick outlets that lean different ways, and notice what each includes, leaves out or emphasises.
  • Separate news from opinion. Check whether you're reading reporting, analysis or a column. Many outlets label them, but in small print.
  • Watch the verbs and adjectives. "Claimed" versus "said", "slammed" versus "criticised": loaded words are the most common sign of slant.
  • Go to the source. For a statistic, ruling or speech, find the original document or transcript when it matters.
  • Learn the background. Context is the best defence against framing. A story that seems outrageous often looks different once you know what came before.

Questions people ask

Is there a truly unbiased news app?

No. Every app makes choices about which stories to show and how. The most useful "unbiased" apps don't claim neutrality. They show you the range of coverage, as Ground News and AllSides do, so you can judge for yourself.

Is Ground News actually unbiased?

Ground News doesn't write news. It labels outlets using third-party ratings, which are judgments built around US politics. It's useful for seeing how coverage differs, as long as you treat its labels as a guide.

What is the best free unbiased news app?

AllSides is free and built entirely around balance. AP News is free and fact-first. Google News's Full Coverage view is free and shows the widest range of outlets.

Are AI news summaries less biased?

They can blend many outlets into one account, which helps. But AI models can still reflect the slant of their sources or make mistakes, so the best ones link clearly to the original reporting.
