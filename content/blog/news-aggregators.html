THE SHORT ANSWER

A news aggregator is an app or website that gathers news from many sources in one place. There are five main types: algorithmic feeds such as Google News, RSS readers such as Feedly, bias-comparison tools such as Ground News, AI summarisers such as Particle, and curated editions such as Apple News or Implica. Pick the type that matches what you want from the news, then the app.

What a news aggregator is

A news aggregator doesn't do its own reporting. It collects articles from many publishers, such as newspapers, broadcasters, magazines and blogs, and presents them in one place. That's the difference from a publisher's own app, like the BBC's or the New York Times', which only shows its own journalism.

Where aggregators differ is in who decides what you see: an algorithm, you, an editor, or an AI model. That choice shapes everything else, from how personalised the feed is to whether it ever ends.

1. Algorithmic feeds

An algorithmic aggregator ranks stories automatically, using what's trending, what you've read before and what you follow. It's the most common type and usually free, paid for by ads or by the company's wider business.

Google News

GOOD FOR The biggest free selection of sources

Google News covers almost every outlet and works on iPhone, Android and the web. Its personalised For you tab learns from your Google activity. Full Coverage shows any story as reported by many outlets, and that view isn't personalised. Price: free.

SmartNews

GOOD FOR Fast headlines with handy local tabs

SmartNews sorts news into swipeable tabs (top stories, world, business, local and more) and needs no account. It's ad-supported and quick. Price: free.

The catch with this type: feeds never end, and an engagement-driven algorithm tends to show you more of what grabs attention, which isn't always what matters.

2. RSS readers

RSS is an old, open standard that lets websites publish a feed of their latest articles. An RSS reader collects the feeds you choose and shows new articles in order. There's no algorithm: you see everything from the sources you pick, and nothing else. It's the most controllable type, and the most work to set up.

Feedly

GOOD FOR A polished RSS reader with AI extras

Feedly is the best-known RSS reader, with apps for phone and web. The free plan follows up to 100 sources. Paid plans add more sources, search and AI features. Price: free; Pro from about $6 a month, billed yearly.

Inoreader

GOOD FOR Power users who want rules and filters

Inoreader has a generous free plan (up to 150 feeds) and paid tiers with rules, filters and keyword monitoring. Price: free; Pro about $7.50 a month, billed yearly.

NetNewsWire

GOOD FOR A free, simple reader on Apple devices

NetNewsWire is a free, open-source RSS reader for Mac, iPhone and iPad, with no accounts, ads or tracking. Price: free.

The catch with this type: you have to know which sources you want, and following many active sites quickly becomes an overwhelming inbox.

3. Bias-comparison tools

These aggregators group articles by story and show how outlets with different political leanings cover it. They're designed to counter filter bubbles. For more options of this type, see our guide to Ground News alternatives.

Ground News

GOOD FOR Seeing coverage gaps between left and right

Ground News shows the left-centre-right split of coverage for each story, flags "Blindspot" stories covered mostly by one side, and rates outlets for bias and factuality. Price: free tier; paid plans up to $99.99 a year.

AllSides

GOOD FOR A free, quick three-sided view

AllSides puts left, centre and right headlines for the day's big stories side by side, with a neutral summary, and publishes media bias ratings. Price: free.

The catch with this type: bias labels are judgments, mostly built around US politics, and showing who covered a story isn't the same as explaining it.

4. AI summarisers

The newest type uses AI models to read many articles about a story and write a single summary. It's fast, and it can blend reporting from several outlets into one readable piece.

Particle

GOOD FOR Skimming many stories quickly

Particle summarises each story from multiple sources and lets you switch between views, such as a simpler explanation or opposing sides. It also adds relevant podcast clips. It's on iPhone, Android and the web. Price: free; Particle+ $2.99 a month.

The catch with this type: AI summaries can drop nuance or get details wrong, so the best ones link clearly to the original reporting.

5. Curated editions

Curated aggregators decide what matters for you. Editors, sometimes helped by AI, pick and organise the stories, much as a newspaper's front page does. You give up some personalisation in exchange for a clear sense of what's important.

Apple News

GOOD FOR Edited headlines and paywalled magazines on Apple devices

Apple News mixes human-edited top stories with personalised picks. Its News+ subscription unlocks hundreds of magazines and several newspapers. It's for Apple devices only. Price: free; News+ $12.99 a month. See whether News+ is worth it.

Flipboard

GOOD FOR Curated magazines around your interests

Flipboard collects stories into topic "magazines" curated by its editors and users, in a flip-through layout. It also lets you follow Mastodon and Threads accounts. Price: free.

Implica (yes, ours)

GOOD FOR The important stories, explained

We make Implica, so read this with that in mind. It gives you a short, curated set of the day's most important stories. Each is built from the reporting of multiple outlets and comes explained: what happened, why it matters and to whom, the background, a timeline and what to watch next. Tap any unfamiliar term for a quick explanation, or ask a question about the story. It uses AI to summarise and explain, and every story lists its sources. It covers world, tech and science news, plus a local edition for Hungary in Hungarian and English, with more local editions coming. Price: free for 14 days, then a subscription.

The catch with this type: someone else is choosing, so if you want every story on a niche topic, pair it with an RSS reader or an algorithmic feed.

How to choose

  • You want everything, free: an algorithmic feed (Google News).
  • You know exactly which sites you trust: an RSS reader (Feedly, Inoreader, NetNewsWire).
  • You worry about only hearing one side: a bias-comparison tool (Ground News, AllSides).
  • You want the gist in seconds: an AI summariser (Particle).
  • You want to know what matters and understand it: a curated edition (Apple News, Implica).

Many people combine two types: a broad feed or RSS reader for their specific interests, and a curated or comparison app for the big picture. For a straight comparison of the most popular apps, see the best news apps in 2026.

Questions people ask

Is Google News a news aggregator?

Yes. Google News doesn't write its own stories. It collects and ranks articles from thousands of publishers, which makes it the world's best-known algorithmic aggregator.

What is the best free news aggregator?

For breadth, Google News. For control, a free RSS reader like NetNewsWire or the free tiers of Feedly and Inoreader. For balance, AllSides. All are free.

Is RSS still used?

Yes. Most news sites, blogs and podcasts still publish RSS feeds, often without advertising it. Podcasts are distributed through RSS, so if you use a podcast app, you're already using it.

What's the difference between a news aggregator and a news app?

"News app" covers both aggregators and publishers' own apps. An aggregator collects stories from many outlets. A publisher's app only shows that publisher's journalism.
