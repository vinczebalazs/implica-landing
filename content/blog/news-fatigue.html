THE SHORT ANSWER

News fatigue is the exhaustion that comes from too much news, too often, with too little sense of what it means. The fix isn't to quit the news. It's to change your news diet: check in less often, choose sources that end, go deeper on fewer stories, and focus on what you can actually understand or act on.

What news fatigue is

News fatigue, sometimes called news burnout, is the feeling of being worn down by the news: overwhelmed by the volume, numbed by repeated crises, anxious after reading, and increasingly tempted to look away. It overlaps with news anxiety, the worry and helplessness that heavy news consumption can trigger, and with information overload, the broader sense of having more information than you can process.

It isn't a medical diagnosis. It's a very common reaction to how news reaches us today: constantly, on the same device as everything else, often stripped of the context that would make it make sense.

You're not alone

  • The Reuters Institute's 2026 Digital News Report found that 42% of people across 48 markets sometimes or often avoid the news, up from 29% in 2017.
  • In its 2025 report, the top reasons avoiders gave were the news's negative effect on their mood (39%) and feeling worn out by the amount of news (31%). Too much coverage of war and of politics followed, each cited by 29%.
  • Younger avoiders were more likely to say the news is hard to understand or doesn't feel relevant to them.
  • As far back as 2017, the American Psychological Association found that 56% of Americans said staying informed caused them stress.

Signs you have news fatigue

  • You feel anxious, angry or low after checking the news, but check again anyway.
  • Headlines blur together, and you couldn't explain the stories you've read.
  • You feel numb to news that would once have shocked you.
  • You've started avoiding the news completely, and feel guilty about it.
  • It's affecting your sleep, focus or mood.

Why the news wears us out

There's no end to it

A newspaper had a last page. A 24-hour feed doesn't, so there's never a moment when you're done and can feel informed.

Crises without context

Breaking news arrives as fragments: an attack, a market drop, a political row. Without background, each fragment feels random and threatening. It's harder to be calm about something you don't understand.

Repetition

The same story is reported again and again across outlets and posts, with small updates. It feels like a lot of news, but much of it is the same news.

Helplessness

Most of what we read about, we can do nothing about. A steady diet of problems you can't influence is a recipe for anxiety.

A healthier news diet

Think of news like food: the problem is rarely news itself, but the quantity, the timing and the quality.

1. Check in on purpose, not by reflex

Pick one or two times a day to catch up, and leave it alone otherwise. Very little news changes what you need to do within a few hours, and major emergencies will still reach you.

2. Choose sources that end

Swap endless feeds for finite formats: a daily newsletter, a radio bulletin, a weekly magazine, or an app with a fixed daily set of stories. Reaching the end is what lets you feel informed and put it down.

3. Go deeper on fewer stories

Understanding three important stories properly (what happened, why it matters, how it got here) is more informative and less stressful than skimming fifty headlines. Context turns alarming fragments into something you can make sense of.

4. Cut the repetition

Turn off most breaking-news notifications. Follow a story through one good update a day rather than every incremental post about it.

5. Make room for what you can act on

Local news, practical news and solutions journalism, reporting on how people are responding to problems and what's working, help counter the sense of helplessness.

6. Watch the time of day

Avoid news first thing in the morning and last thing at night, when it has the most effect on your mood and sleep. If late-night scrolling is the issue, see our guide on how to stop doomscrolling.

Is avoiding the news OK?

Taking a break is fine, and sometimes necessary. But switching off for good has costs: the news affects your money, your health, your vote and your community. The more sustainable answer for most people is a smaller, better news habit, not none at all.

Questions people ask

Is news fatigue real?

It isn't a clinical diagnosis, but it's well documented: large surveys such as the Reuters Institute's show rising numbers of people avoiding the news because it affects their mood or wears them out.

How do I stay informed without anxiety?

Check the news at set times, use sources that end, and choose depth over volume. Knowing the background of a story makes it less unsettling than a stream of disconnected headlines.

How much news should I read a day?

There's no right amount, but for most people one or two focused check-ins a day, of around 10 to 20 minutes in total, is plenty to stay well informed.

What's the difference between news fatigue and doomscrolling?

Doomscrolling is a behaviour: compulsively scrolling through bad news. News fatigue is a state: the exhaustion that heavy news exposure, including doomscrolling, can leave you with.
